01 / Chapter 6 §6.1

Survival: whose risk are we measuring?

Imagine following a batch of batteries. The CDF counts failures so far; survival counts what is still running; hazard looks only at the batteries that made it this far.

Three ways to talk about a continuous lifetime X
QuantityQuestion it answersMath
CDFWhat fraction of the original batch has failed by time t?F(t) = P(X ≤ t)
SurvivalWhat fraction of the original batch is still running at t?S(t) = P(X > t) = 1 − F(t)
HazardAmong survivors at t, how fast is failure probability accumulating?λ(t) = f(t)/S(t), if S(t) > 0
Worked example · Illustrative

If X is exponential with rate 0.2 per hour, then S(t) = e−0.2t and f(t) = 0.2e−0.2t. The exponentials cancel in f/S: the hazard is always 0.2 per hour, even as the surviving fraction shrinks. For a surviving battery the chance of failing in the next 0.1 hour is about 0.02, not 0.2.

Why divide by S(t)? And can we go backwards?

Condition on X > t. The exact next-interval probability is [F(t + Δt) − F(t)]/S(t), which is approximately λ(t)Δt for a short interval. Hazard is a rate, not a probability. Since f = −S′, we have λ = −(log S)′; for a positive lifetime starting with S(0)=1, S(t) = exp(−∫0t λ(u) du).

Build a lifetime distribution from an increasing hazard (§6.1)

Suppose λ(t)=a+bt with a ≥ 0, b > 0 and t ≥ 0. First accumulate the hazard: H(t)=at+bt²/2. Then get survival, failure probability, and density in that order:

S(t)=e−H(t);   F(t)=1−e−H(t);   f(t)=(a+bt)e−H(t).

The conditional failure rate rises as the item ages. The density need not rise: a rising failure rate can be offset by fewer items surviving to that age. For any s ≥ 0, the chance of lasting another s given survival to t is S(t+s)/S(t). With constant hazard c that ratio is e−cs, independent of t. This is exponential memorylessness.

02 / Chapter 6 §§6.2–6.3

Count with 0s and 1s; bound with what you know

Indicators: “Did it happen?”

IA = 1 if A happens and 0 otherwise. Its mean is P(A). To get an expected count, write one indicator per opportunity, then add their probabilities.

Example: In five fair coin tosses, “HH” can start at four positions. Each has probability 1/4, so the expected count is 1. Overlaps count, and linearity of expectation does not require independence.

Inequalities: “How large could the chance be?”

Markov: If X ≥ 0 and a > 0, then P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a. On the event, X contributes at least a to the average.

Chebyshev: If X has mean μ and finite variance σ², then P(|X − μ| ≥ d) ≤ σ²/d² for d > 0. Apply Markov to the squared distance (X − μ)².

Worked example · Illustrative

A nonnegative waiting time has mean 10 minutes and SD 2 minutes. Markov gives P(X ≥ 15) ≤ 10/15. Chebyshev gives P(X ≥ 15) ≤ P(|X − 10| ≥ 5) ≤ 4/25. Neither bound tells you the exact probability.

Indicator shortcuts for AND, OR, and NOT

IA∩B = IAIB;   IA∪B = IA + IB − IAIB;   IAᶜ = 1 − IA.

Indicators can also specify where a density applies: 2x I{0<x<1} means “2x inside (0,1), zero outside.”

Choose what to count: distinct stops, patterns, and runs (§6.2)
  • Distinct stops: If m riders independently choose among n equally likely floors, use one indicator per floor, not per rider. The chance a given floor gets no riders is (1−1/n)m, so the expected number of stops is n[1−(1−1/n)m].
  • Pattern matches: In L independent uniform letters from a 26-letter alphabet, a fixed four-letter word has probability 26−4 at each starting position. For L ≥ 4, the expected number of matches is (L−3)/26⁴, even if the windows overlap.
  • Runs: A run is a maximal block of identical outcomes. The first toss starts one; each later toss starts a new one precisely when it differs from the preceding toss. For n ≥ 1 independent tosses with head probability p, the expected count is 1+(n−1)[p(1−p)+(1−p)p].
Derive both bounds in two steps (§6.3)

Markov: X ≥ aI{X≥a} pointwise: inside the event X is at least a; outside the event the right side is zero. Take expectations to get E[X] ≥ aP(X ≥ a).

Chebyshev: Use the same argument with W=(X−μ)² and cutoff d². Since E[W]=σ², the bound follows. For σ > 0 and d = kσ, it becomes P(|X−μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k². In particular, at least 75% of the probability lies strictly within 2 SDs of the mean, without assuming a bell curve.

03 / Chapter 6 §§6.4–6.5

Quantiles: read the CDF backwards

A CDF takes a cutoff and gives you a probability. A quantile takes a probability and gives you the first cutoff that reaches it.

qp = inf {x : F(x) ≥ p},   0 < p < 1.

Smooth vs. stepped

When F is continuous and strictly increasing through the target, solve F(x)=p. For example, the median of an exponential with rate λ is log(2)/λ. For a discrete CDF, step through the values until you first reach or pass p. If the CDF values at 0, 1, 2 are 0.20, 0.55, 1.00, the median is 1, not a point between 0 and 1.

Mixed: a smooth stretch and a jump

Adapted from Example 6.7: a test records X = min(T, 2), where T is exponential with rate 1 (in the chosen time units). Early failures have density e−x on 0 < x < 2; every device that makes it to 2 is recorded as exactly 2. Thus P(X=2)=e−2≈0.135.

Try the cutoff rule: Just before 2 the mixed CDF approaches 0.865, then jumps to 1 at 2. The 90th percentile is 2 even though the CDF never equals 0.90. To find the mean of this mixed variable, add both contributions: ∫02 xe−x dx + 2e−2 = 1 − e−2.

Calculate normal, exponential, and binomial quantiles (§6.5)
  • Normal: For X ∼ N(μ,σ²) with σ > 0, standardize to find qp=μ+σΦ−1(p). For mean 69 and SD 12, the 85th percentile is approximately 69+12(1.0364)=81.44. The number 1.0364 is a z-score, not a probability.
  • Exponential: Solve 1−e−λx=p to get qp=−log(1−p)/λ. The 90th percentile uses a right-tail probability of 0.10.
  • Binomial: For X ∼ Bin(5,0.3), the cumulative probabilities at 0, 1, and 2 are about 0.1681, 0.5282, and 0.8369. Thus the median is 1 and the 75th percentile is 2. Compare p against the running total, not the mass of one outcome.

Reading notation: The subscript p here is the area to the left of the cutoff (including the cutoff). If a problem defines a critical value by a right-tail area α for a continuous model, look up the left-tail level 1 − α.

04 / Chapter 6 §6.6

Transformations: translate the event before calculating

When Y = g(X), the probabilities have not disappeared. Work out which original X values produce the Y outcomes you want.

  1. Find the possible Y values. Squaring can never produce a negative value.
  2. Translate the cutoff. Start with FY(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y) and rewrite it using X. A decreasing transformation reverses the inequality.
  3. Read off what you need. Differentiate the resulting CDF for a density when Y is absolutely continuous. For discrete Y, add the masses of all X values mapping to each y.
Worked example · Squaring a standard normal (Example 6.16)

For y ≥ 0, P(X² ≤ y) = P(−√y ≤ X ≤ √y) = 2Φ(√y) − 1 by symmetry. For y < 0, it is 0. The density for y > 0 is φ(√y)/√y, where Φ and φ are the standard normal CDF and density. Using only the positive root misses half the probability.

Shortcut for a smooth one-to-one transformation

On the transformed support, with differentiable inverse, fY(y) = fX(g−1(y)) |(g−1)′(y)|, and the density is zero outside. The inverse locates the original value; the absolute derivative adjusts for how much X-width fits inside a small Y interval.

For X ∼ Exp(λ) and Y=X1/β with β > 0, substitute x=yβ to get the Weibull density λβyβ−1e−λyβ for y > 0. The hazard λβyβ−1 decreases for β < 1, is constant at β = 1, and increases for β > 1.

Work through five distribution links in §6.6
  • Uniform → exponential: For U ∼ Uniform(0,1) and Y=−log(U)/λ, the event Y ≤ y becomes U ≥ e−λy for y ≥ 0. Its probability is 1−e−λy. The negative sign is why the inequality flips.
  • Normal → uniform: For standard normal Z, let U=Φ(Z). For 0 < u < 1, P(U ≤ u)=P(Z ≤ Φ−1(u))=u, the uniform CDF. A point’s percentile rank is uniform under this model.
  • Normal → log-normal: For X ∼ N(μ,σ²), Y=eX is positive. Substitute x=log(y) and multiply by the inverse derivative 1/y, giving fY(y)=fX(log y)/y for y > 0.
  • Scaling a gamma: If X has shape α and rate λ, then kX with k > 0 has shape α and rate λ/k. The inverse x = y/k turns e−λx into e−(λ/k)y; the derivative 1/k supplies the remaining factor.
  • Gamma → Maxwell–Boltzmann form: If X has gamma shape 3/2 and rate λ, then for Y=√X the inverse is x=y². The factor x1/2 becomes y and the derivative contributes 2y. Together they give fY(y)=4λ3/2y²e−λy²/√π for y > 0.

The earlier square-of-normal example gives Z² ∼ χ²(1). In contrast, the affine transform μ+σZ (σ > 0) keeps a normal shape while changing its mean and variance to μ and σ².

Joint, marginal, independent: three different questions

Say X and Y record whether a student attended two different review sessions (1 = yes, 0 = no). “Attended both” needs the pair; “attended the first, regardless of the second” needs a marginal.

Table lab · Illustrative probabilities

Read the same table three ways

First sessionY = 0Y = 1Row total

Column total0.600.401.00
  1. Joint: P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.30. Read the cell where both coordinates are 1.
  2. Marginal: P(X = 1) = 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50. Add across the row to include both possibilities for Y.
  3. Independence: 0.30 ≠ 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.20. The actual joint cell differs from the product of its margins, so X and Y are dependent.

Continuous case: replace sums with integrals

The support is the region where the model has density. A joint density is nonnegative and integrates to 1 over the plane. To get a probability, integrate over the region of interest. For the joint CDF F(x,y)=P(X ≤ x, Y ≤ y), use the part of the support below and to the left of (x,y). In the discrete case, sum the cells in that region instead.

To get the marginal for X, hold x fixed and integrate out y. On the pictured triangle, the y bounds are 0 to x, so fX(x)=∫0x 2 dy=2x for 0 < x < 1, and zero elsewhere.

Check the whole model for independence. In a table, check p(x,y)=pX(x)pY(y) for every pair. A single mismatch proves dependence; a single match proves nothing. For densities, check f(x,y)=fX(x)fY(y) almost everywhere, including zero-density regions. Even though the triangle’s density is constant inside it, the variables are dependent: both marginals are positive on (0,1), but pairs with y > x are impossible.

More than two variables and the CDF test

Keep the variables you want and sum or integrate out all the others. The joint distribution determines its marginals, but marginals alone do not generally reconstruct the joint. Mutual independence lets you reconstruct it by multiplying all the marginals. Equivalently, the joint CDF must equal the product of the marginal CDFs at every cutoff vector; pairwise independence alone is not enough.

From sampling to joint PMF, marginal, and CDF: the lecture urn (§7.1)

Draw 3 objects without replacement from 3 red, 4 white, and 5 blue. Let X and Y count the red and white objects. For nonnegative integers x,y with x + y ≤ 3, the joint probability is C(3,x)C(4,y)C(5,3−x−y)/C(12,3), where C(n,k) counts unordered choices. The rest of the table is zero: every draw uses up one of the three slots.

  • One cell: The draw (1 red, 1 white, 1 blue) has p(1,1)=3·4·5/220=60/220.
  • An event: For P(X=Y), sum the diagonal; only (0,0) and (1,1) are possible. The result is (10+60)/220=7/22.
  • One margin: Counting 1 red and any 2 non-red directly gives P(X=1)=3C(9,2)/220=108/220. Summing the row gives the same answer: you are ignoring the white/blue split.
  • Joint CDF: F(1,1) counts four cells: (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), and (1,1), for (10+40+30+60)/220=7/11. It is not the (1,1) cell alone.
Continuous worked example: marginals, joint CDF, and joint density (§7.1)

For f(x,y)=4x(1−y) on the unit square and zero outside, integrate y from 0 to 1 to get fX(x)=2x on [0,1]. Its integral is 1, which also verifies normalization of the joint density. Integrating out x instead gives fY(y)=2(1−y) on [0,1]. Their product equals the joint density on and off the square, so the variables are independent.

For x and y inside the square, use upper limits x and y, not 1: F(x,y)=x²(2y−y²). For example, F(0.5,0.5)=3/16. If either cutoff is negative, F is 0; a cutoff above 1 can be replaced by 1. This gives the CDF on the whole plane.

Where the derivatives exist in the interior, recover the density by differentiating F once in x and once in y. A rectangle probability instead uses four CDF corners: P(a < X ≤ b, c < Y ≤ d)=F(b,d)−F(a,d)−F(b,c)+F(a,c). Add back the bottom-left corner because it was subtracted twice.

Independence traps: rectangular support, disks, and pairwise checks (§7.2)
  • Product-shaped support is not sufficient. The density (x+y)e−x−y/2 on the positive quadrant has margins (x+1)e−x/2 and (y+1)e−y/2. Their product has the factor (x+1)(y+1)/4, not (x+y)/2. Seeing a product of exponentials is not enough when another factor still mixes x and y.
  • Constant density is not sufficient. A point uniform inside the unit disk has joint density 1/π there. At a fixed x with |x| < 1, the vertical slice has length 2√(1−x²), so fX(x)=2√(1−x²)/π. Both coordinates can individually be near 0.9, but not together.
  • Pairwise independence is not mutual independence. Let X and Y be independent fair bits and let Z be 1 when the bits differ. Every pair is independent, but the only way for all three to be 0 is (X,Y)=(0,0), which has probability 1/4, not the product 1/8.
Two more lecture density patterns (§7.1)

Chi-square example: The density e−(x+y)/2/(2π√(xy)) for x,y > 0 splits into two e−x/2/√(2πx)-type factors. Each is a χ²(1) density. The split immediately identifies the marginals and independence.

Bivariate normal example: The lecture density is proportional to exp[−2(x²+xy+y²)/3] on the plane. The term xy prevents the exponent from splitting into a sum of a function of x and a function of y. To integrate out y, complete the square: x²+xy+y²=(y+x/2)²+3x²/4. The shifted Gaussian integral over y is constant in x, leaving a marginal proportional to e−x²/2. Normalization gives the standard normal density; symmetry gives the same marginal for Y. Normal marginals alone do not make a pair independent.

06 / Chapter 7 §§7.3–7.4

Means add. Variances also track how variables move together.

To find E[g(X,Y)], multiply each output g(x,y) by its joint mass and sum over pairs, or integrate g(x,y)f(x,y) over the support. In the table above, XY equals 1 only in the “both” cell, so E[XY]=0.30. But E[X]E[Y]=0.50 × 0.40=0.20.

Covariance: signed co-movement

Center both variables at their means and multiply the deviations. Same-side deviations contribute positively; opposite-side deviations contribute negatively.

Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y].

For the attendance table, covariance is 0.10. Its size depends on the units of X and Y.

Correlation: co-movement on a common scale

Divide by both standard deviations, assuming they are finite and nonzero.

ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σXσY),   −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1.

Values ±1 mean an exact positive or negative straight-line relationship. Shifting or positively rescaling either variable leaves ρ unchanged; reversing one variable’s scale flips the sign.

E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]

Var(aX + bY) = a²Var(X) + b²Var(Y) + 2ab Cov(X,Y)

Neither identity requires independence (assuming the needed moments exist). The variance cross-term is what you pick up when you square the centered sum. For more variables, add one such cross-term per unordered pair.

Independence implies zero covariance, not the reverse. Let X be equally likely to be −1, 0, or 1, and let Y = X². Knowing X tells you Y exactly. Yet E[X]=0 and E[XY]=E[X³]=0 by symmetry, so ρ = 0. Correlation measures linear association, not all kinds of dependence or causation.

Expectation in series and in parallel: when do you need the joint model? (§7.3)

For the resistor pair with joint density (x+y)e−x−y/2 on x,y > 0, series resistance is the sum. Each marginal mean is ½∫0∞x(x+1)e−x dx = 3/2, so the expected series resistance is 3, even though the variables are dependent.

Parallel resistance is xy/(x+y). Multiply by the joint density before integrating: x + y cancels, leaving ½(xe−x)(ye−y). Each one-dimensional integral is 1, so the expected parallel resistance is 0.5. Substituting both marginal means into the parallel formula instead gives 0.75, which is not the answer.

Rule of thumb: A function of X alone only needs X’s marginal; a sum of separate functions can be averaged term by term. A product g(X)h(Y) factors in expectation if X and Y are independent and the means exist. Otherwise, use the joint model unless you have another reason the expectation factors.

Use moment-generating functions to recognize a sum (§7.3)

The MGF is MX(t)=E[etX], where it is finite. For independent X and Y, MaX+bY(t)=MX(at)MY(bt): the exponential of a sum becomes a product, then independence lets you split the expectation.

For independent X ∼ χ²(r), Y ∼ χ²(s), multiply (1−2t)−r/2 and (1−2t)−s/2 for t < 1/2. Their exponents add, giving the MGF of χ²(r+s). Matching MGFs that exist in a neighborhood of zero identifies the distribution. Do not apply this product rule to dependent variables just because their means add.

Compute covariance and correlation from the lecture urn (§7.4)

Use draw positions to avoid summing the whole table. The chance any position is red is 3/12; the chance it is white is 4/12. Thus E[X]=3/4 and E[Y]=1. The product XY counts ordered red-position/white-position pairs. They must be distinct, so there are 3·2 candidates, each with probability (3/12)(4/11). Therefore E[XY]=6/11 and Cov(X,Y)=6/11−3/4=−9/44.

Using the without-replacement variance below for red and white separately gives Var(X)=81/176 and Var(Y)=6/11. Dividing by the product of SDs gives ρ=−1/√6≈−0.408. Red and white compete for sample slots, but blue objects mean that X does not fully determine Y.

Two lecture applications and the reason |ρ| ≤ 1
  • Sampling without replacement: If a fraction p of N objects are red, the count among n draws has mean np by indicators. Drawing red leaves fewer reds for later draws; the pairwise covariance is −p(1−p)/(N−1) for N > 1. The count variance is np(1−p)(N−n)/(N−1), smaller than with replacement if n > 1 and 0 < p < 1.
  • Portfolio risk: For weighted returns wR1+(1−w)R2, combine the means with the weights, but include return covariance when calculating portfolio variance. Lower covariance lowers variance at fixed weights and individual variances when 0 < w < 1.
  • Cauchy–Schwarz: For square-integrable U and V, |E[UV]| ≤ √(E[U²]E[V²]). Apply this to centered X and Y to bound the absolute covariance by the product of the two SDs.
Portfolio weights and the minimum-variance choice (§7.4)

Write v₁=Var(R₁), v₂=Var(R₂), c=Cov(R₁,R₂). With weight w in asset 1, the variance is a quadratic: V(w)=(v₁+v₂−2c)w²+2(c−v₂)w+v₂. If v₁+v₂−2c=Var(R₁−R₂)>0, setting the derivative to zero gives w*=(v₂−c)/(v₁+v₂−2c). If short selling is not allowed, restrict w to [0,1] and use the nearest endpoint if the unconstrained optimum lies outside.

Illustrative arithmetic: If the SDs are 10 and 20 percentage points and covariance is zero, the minimum-variance weight in asset 1 is 400/(100+400)=0.8. The portfolio SD is √(0.8²·100+0.2²·400)=√80≈8.94 percentage points: less than either asset’s SD.

When the two expected returns differ, the portfolio mean changes linearly with w, so variance is also quadratic in the mean. Where two feasible portfolios have the same risk, only the one with higher expected return is efficient. The notes’ utility example, U=μ−kV with k > 0, makes the trade-off explicit: increasing k penalizes risk more.

Why the unit-circle example has zero correlation (§7.4)

Choose an angle Θ uniform from 0 to 2π and set X=cos Θ, Y=sin Θ. This is the circumference, not the uniform disk above. Reflecting across a coordinate axis preserves the distribution but flips one coordinate’s sign. Consequently both means and E[XY] are zero. Yet X²+Y²=1 always, so knowing X restricts Y to at most two values. In particular, both coordinates can separately have absolute value above 0.9, but never together.

07 / Retrieval practice

Pick the tool before doing the algebra

Try each question on paper first. Then reveal the reasoning.

1. Ten percent of devices survive to t, and 2% of the original batch fail in the next hour. What fraction of the survivors fail in that hour?

20%. Those failures come from the survivors, so divide 0.02 by 0.10. This is a one-hour conditional probability, not the instantaneous hazard.

2. An exam has mean 60 and SD 5. Without assuming normality, bound the chance of a mark at least 10 points away from 60.

At most 25%. Chebyshev gives P(|X − 60| ≥ 10) ≤ 5²/10² = 1/4.

3. F(1) = 0.40 and F(2) = 0.85, and X is integer-valued. What is the 75th percentile?

2. At 1 the CDF falls short, and no integer lies between 1 and 2. Look for the first cutoff that reaches or exceeds 0.75.

4. If Y = 10 − X, which X outcomes satisfy Y ≤ 4?

X ≥ 6. The transformation is decreasing, so the inequality reverses. Keep the equality if X might have an atom at 6.

5. Two variables have variances 4 and 9 and covariance −2. What is the variance of their sum?

9. Use 4 + 9 + 2(−2) = 9. The negative covariance offsets some of the individual variability.

6. Four riders choose among three floors independently and uniformly. What is the expected number of stops?

65/27, about 2.41. A given floor is missed with probability (2/3)⁴, so add 1−(2/3)⁴ for each of the three floors. The expectation need not be an integer.

7. For the triangle 0 < y < x < 1 with density 2, find the marginal density of Y and P(Y > 1/2).

fY(y)=2(1−y) for 0 < y < 1, and the probability is 1/4. Use a horizontal slice: at fixed y, x runs from y to 1. Integrate the resulting marginal from 1/2 to 1.

8. Independent X and Y have χ² distributions with 2 and 5 degrees of freedom. What is the distribution of X + Y? What if independence is not given?

χ²(7) under independence. The MGFs multiply. Without independence, the marginal distributions alone do not determine the distribution of the sum.
